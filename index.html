Computational Data Analytics

Three assignments · Weka · benchmark datasets

I investigated how preprocessing, rule learning, classifier choice and clustering affect results, and how interpretable or costly the analysis is.

Classifier comparison on 10 datasets

I compared JRip (with and without pruning) against ConjunctiveRule using 10-fold cross-validation, looking at accuracy, rule count, conditions, predicted classes and runtime. Datasets ranged from labour (57 instances) to connect-4 (67,557). A Friedman test (χ² = 13.55) followed by a Nemenyi post-hoc test showed both JRip variants significantly outperform ConjunctiveRule, while pruned and unpruned JRip do not differ significantly in accuracy. Pruning gave far simpler models.

Discretisation and tree size

Learning the discretisation inside each cross-validation fold avoided leaking test-fold information. The best setup (FilteredClassifier, binary) reached 94.99% accuracy with 11 leaves and 21 nodes, against 94.56% and 27 nodes for the original J48.

Association rules

With Apriori on the weather data, moving from support 0.1 to support 0.2 and confidence 0.9 cut 336 rules to 8. I judged rules by lift and leverage, not confidence alone. On the Adult dataset, FP-Growth was 14–40× faster than Apriori with near-identical rule counts.

Explainable AI: Anchors

Seminar presentation · Ribeiro, Singh & Guestrin (AAAI 2018)

I presented “Anchors: High-Precision Model-Agnostic Explanations”. An anchor is an IF–THEN rule that keeps a model’s prediction stable when other parts of the input change.

What I covered

  • Why black-box models need explanations in medicine, credit scoring and criminal justice.
  • How Anchors differ from LIME: rules with clear coverage instead of a local linear model.
  • How candidate rules are found with beam search and the KL-LUCB bandit method.
  • The paper’s simulated-user experiment and its user study with 26 students.

Takeaway

Anchors gave higher-precision explanations, and users predicted model behaviour more precisely. The trade-off is that stricter explanations apply to fewer cases. I also discussed limits: overly specific or conflicting anchors, complex outputs and dependence on realistic perturbations.

Experiment 1: simulated users

The paper first tests whether anchors help a simulated user predict a model’s behaviour on unseen instances better than LIME. Models are trained on a training set, explanations are made on a validation set and evaluated on a test set. Precision is the share of the user’s predictions that match the model. Coverage is the share of test instances where the explanation applies. Three datasets (adult, rcdv, lending) and three models (logistic regression, 400 gradient-boosted trees, a two-layer neural network) were used. “LIME (n)” applies LIME directly to unseen instances, while “LIME (t)” has a threshold tuned with an oracle to match the anchors’ precision.

Anchors reached 93–100% precision everywhere, against 66–81% for untuned LIME. A tuned LIME covered more instances on adult and rcdv, which is the precision–coverage trade-off, but not on lending.

Experiment 2: user study

26 students who had taken or were taking a machine-learning course predicted a model’s output on unseen instances for the adult and rcdv datasets, plus a visual question-answering system on two images (VQA 1 and VQA 2). They did this with no explanation, with a LIME explanation, or with an anchor. Results below are after the first explanation.

User precision

How often users’ predictions matched the model. Anchors were highest on three of four tasks; on rcdv LIME was marginally higher (98.1% vs 97.8%).

Perceived coverage

Share of instances where users felt confident predicting. LIME made users feel more sure, but less accurate.

Time per prediction

Seconds per prediction. Users were faster with anchors on every task.

Visualising AI Research Sessions for Domain Experts In progress

University project · research and design phase

This project looks at how findings from AI-agent research sessions can be shown to business experts using visual language they already know from analytics, rather than as a raw transcript.

Business context: retail and product sales

Why is a product not selling? Why does a business keep ending up with leftover inventory? What evidence supports or weakens each explanation?

Design considerations

  • Keep the view anchored to the decision-maker’s question.
  • Show the evidence and where it came from.
  • Make relationships between factors visible.
  • Separate what the agent explored from what remains unexplored.
  • Show uncertainty so tentative findings are not read as facts.

So far my work is defining the business problem, researching requirements and designing how findings should be organised. The interface itself is not built yet.
